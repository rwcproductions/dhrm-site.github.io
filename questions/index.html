Common questions

Short answers about the idea, the medicine, and this site.

About the medicine

Can I see a restorative physician?

No. The profession does not exist, and no one holds a Doctor of Hygienic-Restorative Medicine (DHRM). For your own health, please see a qualified clinician. If symptoms are sudden, severe, or getting worse, get urgent care or call emergency services.

Is this alternative medicine?

No. In the thought experiment, a restorative physician is a licensed physician trained in the same sciences as an MD or a DO: anatomy, pathology, microbiology, diagnosis, pharmacology, surgery, obstetrics, and emergency care. It is not naturopathy under another name, and it is not wellness culture with religious vocabulary.

Would a restorative physician avoid drugs or surgery?

No. They would be sparing with drugs, but not careless with life. The question would be what this patient needs right now that does the most good and the least harm. Sometimes that is rest, food, water, instruction, and time. Sometimes it is insulin, antibiotics, or the operating room.

Why “hygienic”?

Because of the word’s older meaning: the ordered conditions of life, such as air, water, food, rest, exercise, sunlight, cleanliness, and temperance. The name keeps that older sense, not the modern one of washing your hands.

What is a sanitarium?

A health institution built around recovery rather than acute crisis. Patients rested, ate well, were nursed and treated, and were taught how to live differently, away for a while from the conditions that helped make them sick. In this alternate history, sanitariums work alongside hospitals rather than replacing them.

About the idea

Is this an Adventist idea?

Yes. It grows out of Seventh-day Adventist medical missionary work, and out of Ellen G. White’s writings on health, education, and medical work, which shaped that work from the start. You do not need to be Adventist to follow it, and the site does not assume you are.

Is this a prediction or a proposal?

Neither. It is a counterfactual: a disciplined look at what could plausibly have happened if events had gone differently. It does not claim that God guaranteed such an outcome, that Ellen White predicted this profession, or that it would have fixed healthcare. The imagined branch is a mirror, not the authority.

Why imagine a profession that never existed?

Because a road not taken can show what the road actually taken left behind. Seen as one working whole, the pieces of the Adventist healing vision are easier to recognize when they turn up apart: the physician as teacher, the institution as a school of health, care for the whole person, and service to the poor.

Couldn't such a branch have gone wrong too?

Certainly. It could have blamed patients for their illnesses, controlled them instead of caring for them, made an idol of simple remedies, leaned on weak evidence, turned into a brand, or grown proud. The approach page says more about each.

About this site

Who writes this?

Ricky Curtice, a lifelong Seventh-day Adventist and an engineer by trade. He is not a physician. See about this site.

Is anything here medical advice?

No. The site describes an imagined profession historically, theologically, and as a thought experiment. It does not diagnose, treat, or advise anyone about their own health.
